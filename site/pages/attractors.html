Strange attractors

Four deterministic systems of three coupled ordinary differential equations whose trajectories never settle down: instead they fold back on themselves through an infinite braid that traces a fractal of dimension strictly between 2 and 3. Each one is computed by a 4th-order Runge-Kutta integrator (much more accurate than Euler at the same step size, which matters for stiff systems like Lorenz near its saddle point) and projected to 2D so the visitor can watch it burn onto the canvas.

The full state of the simulation — attractor, palette, seed, step size, rotation, zoom — is encoded in the URL hash so any combination reproduces itself as a shareable link. The "copy link" button copies it to the clipboard. The four systems on offer:

  • Lorenz — the 1963 atmospheric-convection paper. Two-lobed butterfly shape; the orbit switches lobes at seemingly random times.
  • Rössler — simpler than Lorenz, with one slow direction and one fast "fold". Produces a single scroll that wraps around and grows inward.
  • Aizawa — a 1991 system with spherical attractor topology. Looks like a fuzzy sphere with a swirling tunnel through the middle.
  • Halvorsen — a 1993 system with three-fold rotational symmetry; the iconic trefoil shape.

The integrator is RK4 with a fixed step of 0.008 × stepScale per iteration. Each frame runs 16 iterations and overlays the latest segment onto a trail canvas that fades toward the page background by 4% per frame. The fade rate balances "old trajectory disappears eventually" against "new trajectory is visible immediately". A long-burn trajectory naturally accumulates brightness where it spends the most time — the visual signature of an attractor is where the trajectory spends time, not where it goes.
